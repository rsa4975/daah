Reflection on Digital Literacy: Navigating Interdisciplinary Boundaries, Computational Thinking, and AI
Student Profile: 20-year-old Undergraduate Dual-Majoring in Communication & Media Studies and Computer Science

Course: Introduction to Digital Humanities

The Landscape of Everyday Digital Practice
Growing up in a saturated digital ecosystem, my baseline understanding of digital literacy initially felt almost instinctive. In my daily academic and personal routine, content creation and social interaction rely on a suite of familiar tools. I routinely move between platforms like Figma for wireframing media projects, Adobe Creative Cloud for image and video editing, and Markdown-based editors for drafting essays. On the technical side, my computer science background has introduced me to platforms like GitHub for version control and collaborative code management, along with Jupyter Notebooks, RStudio, and Python libraries (such as Pandas, NumPy, and Matplotlib) for data manipulation, statistical analysis, and data visualization.

However, prior to exploring digital humanities, my approach to these technologies was highly siloed. In Computer Science, data was treated as an objective sequence of inputs to be processed and optimized. In Communication and Media, digital spaces were evaluated primarily through qualitative critical theory, audience reception, and narrative structure. I possessed functional literacy—the ability to write scripts, curate social feeds, run regressions, and manage datasets—but I rarely examined how the underlying architectures of these software tools shape the very knowledge they produce.

Interdisciplinary convergence allows us to blend algorithmic logic, critical tool analysis, and qualitative narrative theory into a unified framework.

Interdisciplinary Synergy and Computational Thinking
One of the most transformative elements of this course has been sharing a classroom with peers from vastly different disciplines—ranging from Literature and Film Studies to Interactive Media and Environmental Engineering. Working alongside students with distinct skill sets highlighted that "digital literacy" is far from uniform.

For instance, a peer majoring in Literature brought a sharp capacity for close reading and historical contextualization, noticing nuance, ambiguity, and silences within text archives that my algorithmic scripts would otherwise smooth over. Conversely, my experience with computational thinking—breaking complex systems down into discrete logic, identifying algorithmic patterns, and abstracting data—offered new mechanics for scaling those literary insights across thousands of digitized texts.

This collaboration reframed computational thinking for me. It is not merely a method for making machines execute tasks efficiently; it is a problem-solving orientation that allows us to ask generative questions across domains. When an Environmental Studies student, a Film major, and a Computer Science major look at a single dataset—such as satellite imagery or historical archival logs—each brings a distinct perspective:

Environmental Science: Focuses on spatial metrics, physical change over time, and ecological impact.

Film & New Media: Identifies visual rhetoric, framing, temporal editing, and narrative construction.

Computer Science: Analyzes data schemas, computational load, algorithmic bias, and pipeline efficiency.

Digital Humanities acts as the common ground where these perspectives collide. It encourages us to blend quantitative computational power with qualitative critique, ensuring that while we use data to model the world, we do not lose sight of the human contexts that generated that data in the first place.

New Horizons: Data, AI, and Critical Humanities
As artificial intelligence models—particularly large language models (LLMs) and generative visual architecture—become embedded in academic workflows, the definition of digital literacy must evolve beyond basic software fluency. Digital Humanities offers a vital critical lens for approaching AI. Rather than viewing generative AI as either a magic box or a threat to traditional scholarship, DH models treat it as an object of humanistic inquiry and cultural analytics.

In Data Processing, traditional paradigms prioritize optimization, classification, and execution speed. Digital Humanities shifts this focus toward interrogating data provenance, understanding archives, and recognizing missing or marginalized records.

When considering Artificial Intelligence, a conventional approach often means accepting black-box outputs to automate repetitive tasks. A Digital Humanities perspective requires analyzing training set biases, ethical implications, and broader linguistic shifts caused by generative tools.

Regarding Digital Tools, standard tech views treat software as neutral utilities for content creation. The critical humanities reveal them to be cultural artifacts carrying built-in ideological affordances and implicit design choices.

This perspective challenges me to re-evaluate my current capabilities. While I feel comfortable writing scripts and managing data pipelines, I have discovered a gap in my ability to critically evaluate data provenance and the hidden biases of software affordances. I spent years learning how to build software, but far less time asking why specific technical choices were made or whose voices were left out of the training corpus.

Personal Evaluation and Future Directions
Moving forward, I want to explore research methods that bridge text mining, cultural analytics, and ethical AI development. I aim to move away from using computational tools purely for task automation or statistical prediction, and toward using them as interpretive lenses that surface new historical and cultural questions. True digital literacy requires more than technical proficiency; it demands the critical awareness to question the digital infrastructure shaping our research, communication, and daily lives.

Walking into this course, I assumed "digital literacy" meant knowing how to use software competently — Excel formulas, maybe some Python, formatting a citation correctly. Three weeks in, I've realized that's a narrow slice of a much bigger picture. Digital literacy, as this class frames it, is less about tool fluency and more about a way of thinking: knowing what a tool assumes about the world, what it makes visible or invisible, and how to interrogate a dataset or an algorithm the same way I'd interrogate a source in an argumentative essay.

What I already knew how to do. Looking honestly at my daily habits, I already practice a version of digital literacy without naming it that way. For content creation, I keep a personal Notion workspace where I draft field notes from environmental surveys, and I've gotten comfortable moving between Google Docs (collaborative drafting), Canva (quick infographics for club presentations), and Adobe Premiere Rush (short videos for our sustainability Instagram). For social interaction, I curate — sometimes without thinking about it — which platforms I use for which register: GroupMe for logistics, Discord for our research team's ongoing chatter, LinkedIn for anything I'd want a future employer to see. For research and data management, I lean on Zotero for citations, Google Sheets for smaller datasets, and R (specifically RStudio with the tidyverse) for cleaning and visualizing environmental sensor data. None of this felt like "digital literacy" before this course; it felt like just getting work done.

What the classroom's diversity revealed. The most disorienting and clarifying part of this course has been realizing how differently my classmates relate to the same tools. A Computer Science major in my group treats a dataset as something to be queried and optimized; an Interactive Media student treats the same dataset as raw material for an interactive visualization meant to provoke an emotional response; a Creative Writing minor in our pod wanted to turn our water-quality data into a narrative essay, arguing that numbers alone don't move people to act. Watching these approaches collide in a single project made me realize that "data" isn't a neutral input — it's already an interpretive choice about what counts as evidence, and each discipline brings its own default assumptions about that. Digital humanities, as a field, seems to sit precisely at that friction point: it takes humanistic questions (whose voice is represented, what story is being told, what's excluded) and applies them to computational methods (text mining, network analysis, GIS mapping) that I'd previously associated only with STEM.

Computational thinking through collaboration. I used to think computational thinking meant writing efficient code. Working across majors has reframed it for me as something closer to decomposition and pattern recognition applied to any kind of problem — including qualitative ones. When our Film and New Media teammate needed to structure interview transcripts for a documentary, she was doing a version of computational thinking: breaking an unwieldy, unstructured problem into taggable, searchable units. I hadn't thought of tagging and coding qualitative data as "computational" before, but it clearly is, and it's made me more comfortable importing methods from environmental modeling into contexts that have nothing to do with the environment.

Where AI fits, and where I'm still uneasy. I use AI tools daily — for summarizing dense policy PDFs, for brainstorming visualization approaches, occasionally for debugging R code. What this course has sharpened is my skepticism about treating AI output as a finished answer rather than a draft to interrogate. I'm still working out how to evaluate an AI-generated summary of a scientific paper with the same rigor I'd apply to a human-written one, and I don't yet have a confident framework for that.

What I want to explore next. I'd like to get more comfortable with text-as-data methods — the kind of distant reading techniques the Literature majors in our cohort use — and apply them to environmental policy documents, which are text-heavy but rarely analyzed that way in my field. I also want to get better at building interactive visualizations rather than static charts, since the Interactive Media students in our group have shown me how much more a dataset can communicate when someone can explore it themselves. Mostly, though, I'm leaving this unit with a more humble sense of what "literacy" means here: not fluency in one toolkit, but the flexibility to borrow methods across disciplines and ask what each one reveals or hides.

As a 20-year-old university student majoring in Computer Science, I have always thought of myself as someone who is digitally literate. I use technology every day for school, communication, entertainment, and personal projects, so I usually do not think twice about whether I know how to use digital tools. However, this course has made me realize that digital literacy is much bigger than simply knowing how to use a computer or an application. It also involves understanding how information is created, organized, analyzed, and shared, and how technology changes the way we understand the world.

In my academic life, I already use many digital tools without necessarily thinking of them as part of digital literacy. I use Google Drive and Microsoft Word to organize and write assignments, PowerPoint for presentations, and Google Scholar and library databases to find academic sources. I also use spreadsheets such as Excel or Google Sheets to organize information and perform basic calculations. For Computer Science, I use programming environments and GitHub to write, store, and manage code. These tools have taught me some basic research data management skills, such as organizing files, naming documents properly, keeping different versions of my work, and making sure that information can be found again later. For content creation, I am also familiar with editing images, presentations, and videos using different software. Socially, I use messaging platforms and social media to communicate, share information, and collaborate with other people.

At the same time, I have realized that being comfortable with technology does not mean that I understand everything happening behind it. One of the most interesting parts of digital humanities is that it combines technology with subjects that I might normally see as separate from Computer Science. For example, tools such as the Google Books Ngram Viewer allow researchers to study language and culture through large amounts of digital text. Instead of reading every book individually, researchers can use data to identify patterns in how words and ideas change over time. This changes what can be studied in subjects such as history, literature, and culture. It shows me that computer science is not only about programming. It can also be used as a way of asking new questions about people and society.

The interdisciplinary nature of the class is also important. Students studying Computer Science, Interactive Media, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, Literature, or Creative Writing may approach the same digital tool in completely different ways. A Computer Science student might focus on how a program works, while a Literature student might focus on what the data says about language. An Environmental Studies student could use data to understand changes in the environment, while a Film student could think about how digital tools change storytelling. Working with people from different backgrounds can therefore develop computational thinking because it forces me to explain my ideas in ways that make sense to someone who does not have the same technical knowledge.

I think this is one of the areas where I still have the most to learn. I am comfortable with technology, but I sometimes approach problems from the perspective of what I already know. Collaboration can challenge that habit. Someone from a different discipline may notice a pattern or ask a question that I would not have considered. This makes computational thinking less about simply writing code and more about breaking a complicated problem into smaller parts, finding patterns, testing ideas, and thinking about different possible solutions.

AI is another area where my understanding is developing. I already use AI tools for brainstorming, explaining difficult concepts, and sometimes helping me understand code. However, this course has made me think more critically about where the information produced by AI comes from and what limitations it has. AI is not simply a machine that gives us correct answers. It depends on data, models, and human decisions. This means that digital literacy also requires knowing when to trust a tool, when to question its results, and when to verify information independently.

Overall, I would describe my current digital literacy as strong in everyday technology but still developing in critical and computational thinking. I know how to use many digital platforms, but I want to understand more about what happens underneath them and how digital methods can change the questions I ask. I would especially like to explore data visualization, text analysis, AI, and digital methods for studying culture and society. Digital humanities gives me a way to connect my Computer Science background with subjects outside of technology. Instead of seeing technology as just a tool for completing work, I am beginning to see it as a way of discovering completely new ways of looking at information, people, and the world.
